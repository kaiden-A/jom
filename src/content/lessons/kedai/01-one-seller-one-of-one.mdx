You have one chair. Two people want it. Who gets it?

That is the whole project. Not a catalog with infinite stock — a shelf with **one of each**. The moment you accept that, a different set of problems appears. A normal shop subtracts from a quantity. A personal shop has to decide who is holding the only chair, for how long, and what happens when they disappear.

Kedai is a one-person marketplace for exactly that: the things you are ready to let go. You list an item, share its link, a buyer reserves it, and you arrange payment and pickup on WhatsApp. No customer accounts. No cart. No online payment. One seller, one shelf, one of each.

## Why "Kedai"?

A kedai is a small neighbourhood shop. It has a window, a counter, and a back room. That is the entire architecture of this project:

- **The shop window** is public. Anyone can look at the shelf and open an item link.
- **The counter** is where a buyer reserves one item. It is public too, but it is constrained.
- **The back room** is yours. Publish, price, confirm, cancel, archive. Nobody else gets in.

The name is not decoration. It is the mental model. Every screen in the app belongs to one of those three spaces, and each space has a different permission.

## The three spaces

Here is the model to keep for the rest of the project. Everything else is implementation detail.

<ThreeSpaces client:visible />

The important part is the **asymmetry**:

- The shop window is a public read. It is marketing.
- The counter is a public write, but it can only create **one pending reservation per item**. It cannot confirm a sale.
- The back room is an authorized write. It is the only place that can turn a reservation into a sale.

If you remember one sentence from this lesson, make it this one:

> **The counter can hold the chair. Only the back room can sell it.**

## The item lifecycle

An item is not a product with a stock count. It is a single object moving through a small set of states:

```text
draft ──publish──▶ available ──buyer reserves──▶ reserved ──seller confirms──▶ sold
                       ▲                              │
                       │                              ├──seller cancels──┐
                       │                              └──hold expires────┤
                       └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Two rules matter more than the diagram:

- **A pending reservation is a hold, not a sale.** The item is reserved, the buyer has a reference, and the seller still has to confirm the money.
- **A hold has a deadline.** After 24 hours, the reservation expires and the item returns to the shelf. A shop that forgets to release stale holds slowly stops selling.

We will build both as database constraints and conditional updates, not as `if` statements scattered through the UI.

## What is actually hard here

Uploading a photo and listing an item is the easy part. The Gallery Blog project already taught that. Kedai adds four problems that are mostly about **state**, not UI:

1. **The stock is one.** Two buyers can click reserve at the same time. One must win; the other must see an honest “just reserved” state. A quantity count cannot save you here.
2. **The hold expires.** Time is part of the state. A reservation that is pending forever is a shop that never sells the chair again.
3. **The price must be frozen.** An item can cost RM 180 today and RM 150 next week. A reservation created today must still be RM 180 forever.
4. **Only the seller can confirm.** A buyer's claim is useful information, not a payment. The interface must make the difference impossible to miss.

That is why the database is not just a list of items. It is the source of truth for **who is holding which item, until when, and why**.

## The state machine

The reservation is the heart of the system. It only moves in a few directions, and every move has a guard.

```text
pending ──seller confirms──▶ confirmed
   │
   ├──buyer or seller cancels──▶ cancelled
   └──24 hours pass────────────▶ expired
```

The item moves with it:

```text
available ──reserve──▶ reserved ──confirm──▶ sold
     ▲                     │
     └──cancel or expire───┘
```

Two rules matter more than the diagram:

- **Only `pending` can become `confirmed`.** Not `cancelled`, not `expired`, not `confirmed` again.
- **Email status is separate from reservation status.** A confirmed sale with a failed receipt is still confirmed. The buyer is entitled to the item; the email is just how they find out.

We will build both as explicit database constraints and conditional updates, not as promises in a component.

## What we are not building

<Callout variant="warn" title="Scope is a feature">
No customer accounts. No cart. No multi-vendor marketplace. No subscriptions. No Stripe checkout. No reviews. No search. No shipping calculator. Each one is a real product decision, and each one would make the first version harder to finish and easier to get wrong.

The manual WhatsApp workflow is not a limitation we are apologising for. It is the workflow many small shops actually use. The project is about making that workflow **safe**, not replacing it with a payment gateway.
</Callout>

This matters because the interesting engineering is not in the shop UI. It is in the moment two people want the same chair, and in the moment the first one disappears.

## The five promises

These are the invariants the rest of the project will protect. We will turn them into database constraints, conditional updates, and tests.

1. **The price comes from the item.** The browser never sends an amount.
2. **One item has at most one pending reservation.** The database enforces it.
3. **Only the seller can confirm a sale.** A buyer's claim is recorded, not trusted.
4. **An expired hold releases the item.** Time is part of the state.
5. **An item with history is archived, never hard-deleted.** The shop keeps its memory.
